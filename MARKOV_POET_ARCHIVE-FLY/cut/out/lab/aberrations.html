THE ABERRATIONS

Every shot in the archive was parsed and nothing was thrown away. The hand-typed lexicon and a panoptic model were asked to describe the same 135 pictures; they agree on 91 of 135. What follows is the other 44 — not an error rate, a map of where two describers of one object diverge. These are the rows worth having found.

ONE · The figure that reads as weather

The lexicon types these POET. The model does not find a person in them at all — it finds sky. Three of the four are NEON SKELETON: a body drawn in light against black, which to something trained on photographs of the world is not a man, it is a night. This is also the shot the earliest cuts leaned on hardest and were told off for. The most over-used picture in the archive turns out to be the one picture in it that cannot be seen as a person.

THREE · The subject that will not hold still

Coverage measured at three moments in the shot, and the swing between them. A one-point-oh swing means the figure is the entire frame at one moment and absent at another. These are not bad parses — they are pictures in which the question how much of this is the subject has no stable answer. A boundary that breathes is a dissolve the footage computed for itself.

FOUR · Nouns this world has no word for

The model's vocabulary is COCO's: eighty things photographed in ordinary life. Turned on a generated film about leaving, it reaches for the nearest domestic object it knows and finds cake, and a vase, and birds. Nothing in the poems is a cake. These are not detections, they are the model's own furniture showing through — and they are the only nouns in this project that neither the poet nor the lexicon chose.
