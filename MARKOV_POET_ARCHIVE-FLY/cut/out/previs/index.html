PREVIS — the halfworld blocks it, the archive fills it

The eighty movements are already staged: THE FALL, HARBOR STREETS, WALKING ON WATER, COLUMNS AND BEARS. Somebody blocked those. And for every body in every frame the runtime hands over exactly what a previs engine needs:

{ guise, mode, face, foot: [x, y], height }

foot is where the body stands in the 192×144 field, height is how tall it is there, face is which way it looks. That is a camera-space transform, given away free, for a figure that is already walking. So the drawn body does not have to be kept — it can be replaced, at its own position, at its own scale, facing its own way, by a photographed person cut out of the film.

  • the halfworld → blocking, timing, staging, camera
  • the archive → the actual bodies, windows, water, buildings
  • beflix → the one surface that lets those two share a frame

COLOUR COMES BACK, AND IT IS MEASURED

Every version before this was cream, ink and one accent — the suite's law, and the reason fourteen films look like one film. For previs that is wrong: a board has to be memorable. So each poem's key is taken by k-means from its own footage.

The first attempt returned lavender and slate for every single poem, because ranking clusters by SIZE always finds the grey: in tone-mapped night footage the biggest cluster is the dark. Ranked by saturation × size instead — the colour that is most itself, weighted by how much of the film it covers — the fourteen separate immediately: MORE HAZE at teal, THE GHOST WAS SEVEN at orange, TWO SETS OF FOOTSTEPS at crimson, I CHOOSE THE DOOR at red.

IDENTITY IS STABLE

A body keeps the same photographed person for as long as it exists, keyed on its footing and guise, so nobody flickers between takes mid-stride. Figures are drawn back to front by depth, so a nearer body occludes a further one. And the set is built from nouns chosen by the MOVEMENT's own name — a movement called HARBOR STREETS is assembled from water, buildings and sky; one called BLINDS UP from windows, walls and a bed.
