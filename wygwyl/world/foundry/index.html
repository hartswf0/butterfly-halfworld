The Foundry — worlds read against the poems, and against a builder

“Our language can be seen as an ancient city: a maze of little streets and squares, of old and new houses, and of houses with additions from various periods; and this surrounded by a multitude of new boroughs with straight regular streets and uniform houses.” — Wittgenstein, §18

Every world is grown in the poems’ order: the narrative (three realms, the journey’s walks, ride, fall, flight, cuts), then the raw grid — each city a patchwork of districts with their own grain (a core, a waterfront with piers, rowhouse boroughs, an old-town maze, curving suburbs, industry) joined by arterials, with older and newer epochs laid over it — then a topography fitted to it or laid first, then nature (field belts, forests, dunes, marsh, rock). At each poem a camera stand turns to hold what he says he sees; what the stand can’t see, the world may grow into view. Then two readers: the poems (is it shootable and playable as the film?) and a builder (would anyone actually build this — streets you can drive up, blocks you can build on, one network, dry ground?).
