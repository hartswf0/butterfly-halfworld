WHERE YOU GO WHEN YOU LEAVE · TITLE SEQUENCE, FIVE WAYS Four in the manner of John Whitney, and the one that plays.

Fourteen films need one thing in front of them. A through D take Whitney's own rule — that a title is never typed on, it is what a field of moving elements looks like at the instant their motion agrees with itself — and each is a single differential law run for its own thirty seconds, the words read out of the law's resonant moments rather than drawn over the top of them.

They are beautiful and they were the wrong thing to put in front of these poems. A harmonic field is busy where the poems are spare; it announces its own cleverness; and its only relation to the films behind it was a few icons scattered on a rotation. E is the answer to that: no field, no law, no mathematics on display. A man in a dim room with a window he does not take, the room lifting away, a long walk past fourteen small marks on the horizon, and a door that opens onto paper. It is the suite in one minute, told with the same marks the films are made of — and it is the one the suite plays.

All five are the real films, running live below, not thumbnails — the same drawPreview the fourteen-film index uses. Open any card for its own page, transport and sound.

THE FIVE

A · The Rosette. One dense field on differential phase — point k turns k times the base rate about its own anchor. Nothing else moves it; the loops, flowers and caustics are what nineteen hundred clocks running at nineteen hundred different speeds look like from above, and the title is a cell lifted from that same field at the moment it resonates.

B · The Arabesque. Six rings, each turning at an integer multiple of one rate and alternating direction, breathing in and out of phase with each other. The two lines of the title are built by two different generations of rings collapsing inward in turn, while the rest of the arabesque keeps running behind them.

C · The Catalogue. A grid of independent Lissajous cells, each one's frequency ratio drifting by a constant amount every step until it passes an integer and the figure snaps to a closed loop — a resonance solved backward so it lands on a chosen instant. Letter by letter, left to right, a cell's loop is swapped for its own slice of the word and stays.

D · The Aura. B and C fused, and the one that plays at the head of the suite. The rings are the ground and never stop; the Lissajous cells live on them rather than in a grid, and each cell's frequency pair is derived from the ring it rides — so it is still one law read at two distances. What neither of the others had: at each resonance the field also throws up an image from the films behind it — the tambourine, the rose window, the moon, the temple, the eleven-petalled daisy, the burning hourglass, the ride, the candle, the named stars. They arrive faint, hold for a breath, and the rotation takes them back. If you have seen the films they are memories; if you have not they are ornament, and both readings are correct.

E · The Walk. The one that plays. Read the fourteen poems end to end and they make one sentence: a man is in a room with a window he does not take, he leaves, he walks past a storm and a shore and a city and a stage and a dance floor and three phone calls and a road and a temple and a reunion and a harbor, and at the end of all of it there is a door, and this time he goes through the door. Five movements, long holds, nothing spins, and the only thing that ever crosses the frame is a man. The picture is built out of tone rather than line — a room here is not a rectangle in outline floating on cream, which is a diagram, but a wall: a flat field of ink with a hole in it where the window is, and the hole is paper. Light falls off from that hole in whole levels, because whole levels are the only falloff this lattice has. The whole story is legible as light: he is in the dark, he walks in the cream, he arrives at a dark wall, and the door opens onto paper. The one accent mark in the sequence is the blue lying across that threshold. It carries no bed from the record — the title sits before the record starts, so the first poem's music no longer plays underneath it — and it ends on the names.
