the city fourteen poems built — a Nolli plan generated from the readings, on Plato’s ring plan

HOW THE POEMS DESIGN THE CITY

Nothing here was placed by hand. Every building is a word as the poet spoke it, its frontage as long as he held it — one unit of the plan is one second of his voice. Words he runs together fuse into a block; a short pause is an alley; a breath is a piazza; a line break is a cross street. The white of the plan — streets, alleys, piazzas — is exactly where he breathes: the city’s public realm is made of his silence.

As in Giambattista Nolli’s 1748 plan of Rome, public interiors are drawn white and numbered. Here a building is public when six or more other mouths sing the same line — consensus makes the commons. They are indexed below.

The macro form is Plato’s Atlantis (Critias 113–117): an island, rings of land and water, a canal across the plain to the sea, mountains to the north. The zones come from the album’s own earlier map (ANT/MAPS): Baltimere — sea-edge memories, personal origins — is the outer ring; the Center — moral crucible, repair, recurrence — the inner ring; Atlantis — false heaven, social illusions, techno-faith — the island; Magic Ride, the night highway, lines the canal. The ring widths are not Plato’s: they fall where the poems’ seconds put them, which is why the island is dense and the home ring thin. Each district reads as a walk, row by row, back and forth. Tap any building to hear that phrase.
